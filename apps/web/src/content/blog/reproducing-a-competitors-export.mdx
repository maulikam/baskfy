Before writing a single factor, we took a 271-row CSV export from an existing momentum screener
and tried to reproduce every number in it. Not to copy it — to find out whether our reading of the
formulas was right, and to find out where the published definitions were ambiguous.

## What reproduced exactly

Most of it. The absolute returns, the RSI levels, the volatilities and the betas all fall out of
the stated definitions once two things are pinned down: that the windows are calendar windows
rather than bar counts, and that volatility is reported as a decimal fraction rather than as a
percentage. The export itself settled the second question, since two of our own documents
disagreed about it.

The blend factors — the averages of two to five window returns — reproduce exactly when the sum is
taken in decimal arithmetic. Taken in floating point, 48 rank inversions become 53. That is not a
formula disagreement; it is a reminder that a ranking built on rounded inputs is sensitive to how
you add them up.

## What did not

**Row order, to within a rounding granularity.** The export carries values rounded to two decimal
places, and the product that produced it ranked on the unrounded ones. 114 of the 271 positions
differ from the file — every one of them inside that granularity. This is not reproducible in
principle from the file alone, and the export's own documentation says so.

**One error figure.** The maximum Sharpe disagreement re-derives as 0.01 rather than the 0.0051
recorded when the file was first analysed. The error distribution has nothing at all between 0.00
and 0.01, which is the signature of re-deriving from already-rounded inputs rather than of a wrong
formula.

## What the exercise actually bought

Two inferred definitions, flagged as inferred: the skip-month construction and the circuit-hit
detection rule. Neither is stated anywhere public, both were reconstructed to fit the observed
values, and both are labelled in our own specification as reconstructions rather than as facts.

It also bought a permanent test. The export is committed, and the parity check that reproduces
every numeric column of all 271 rows is written and runs the moment a real price history is
available to run it against. Until then it skips with its reason attached rather than passing
vacuously — a green test that checks nothing is worse than a red one.

## The uncomfortable part

Reproducing someone else's output is a good way to calibrate and a terrible way to validate. If
their formula is wrong and ours agrees with it, the agreement tells you nothing. Everything here
was cross-checked against the stated definition first and against the export second, in that
order, and where the two conflicted the definition won.
